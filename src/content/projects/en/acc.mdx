## The situation

A university research group needed continuity of serious CPU-based HPC
capacity during a period when its long-term on-premises environment
wasn't available — a documented six-month gap, expected to transition
to cloud-as-burst-capacity once on-prem computing returned. The users
were 10-20 concurrent researchers running a mix of tightly-coupled MPI
applications (molecular dynamics, computational chemistry, atmospheric
and ocean modeling) and high-memory single-node workloads (genomics,
data science), all needing a shared, familiar scientific software
stack, secure institutional access, and real spend control.

## Constraints

- Designed for operation by a very small administrative team — favoring managed orchestration and automation over manually assembled infrastructure
- A defined six-month production lifecycle, not a permanent data center — favoring pay-as-you-go, scale-to-zero capacity over fixed reservations
- Mixed workload profile: both multi-node MPI jobs and memory-heavy single-node jobs needed to coexist under one scheduling policy
- Required institutional-grade security: private network access only, institutional identity integration, and multi-factor authentication
- A fixed, capped project budget, requiring active capacity planning rather than treating compute as an unconstrained resource

## Approach

I designed the full solution — cluster architecture, Slurm scheduling
policy, storage layout, identity model, budget controls, and the
scientific software stack — with Azure-side implementation carried out
by the university's cloud infrastructure team based on my
specifications.

## Key decisions

**Azure CycleCloud instead of reproducing the existing AWS ParallelCluster design.**
The prior environment already ran on AWS with a comparable Slurm-based
architecture. Rather than rebuilding that on Azure by default, I
evaluated CycleCloud on its own merits: it matched the required
orchestration and autoscaling needs and integrated more directly with
the institution's existing identity, storage, and cost-control
services. Cost modeling was one input among several — platform fit and
operational simplicity for a small admin team were the deciding
factors, not price alone.

**A homogeneous compute pool with workload-shaped scheduling, instead of dedicated hardware per workload type.**
Rather than splitting the cluster into separate pools of hardware for
serial, high-memory, and multi-node jobs, I kept the compute nodes
homogeneous and instead redesigned the Slurm policy around four
overlapping partitions with per-project quotas, fair-share priority,
and enforceable core-hour budgets. This preserved full flexibility to
run any job type on any node, while still giving small jobs and large
MPI jobs appropriate scheduling behavior without permanently reserving
capacity for either.

**Reduced peak capacity to stay within budget after costs came in higher than modeled.**
Regional pricing and storage costs ended up higher than the original
estimate. Rather than requesting a larger budget or cutting capacity
sharply, I reduced peak capacity by roughly 11% and tracked early
utilization closely — betting correctly that onboarding-phase usage
wouldn't need full capacity from day one, preserving nearly all of the
platform's scientific capability within the approved budget.

**Centrally managed scientific software and reusable "golden" environments, instead of per-user or fully baked-into-the-image installs.**
I considered two simpler alternatives: let each researcher manage their
own software stack (maximum flexibility, but slow onboarding and
inconsistent results across projects), or bake everything into the
node image (fast for users, but brittle — every update requires
rebuilding and re-coordinating the image). Instead, I built a shared
platform using Spack and Lmod modules, distributed via shared storage
so every autoscaled node sees the same validated stack, plus reusable
"golden" environments for data science and machine learning. This gave
researchers a working, reproducible environment from day one, without
the maintenance fragility of the image-based approach or the
inconsistency of everyone building their own.

## Outcome

The platform is deployed and validated: CycleCloud-managed Slurm,
autoscaling compute, persistent home storage, high-performance parallel
scratch, institutional identity and MFA, accounting, and monitoring are
all in production. Multi-node MPI over the InfiniBand backend has been
benchmarked at roughly 23,584 MB/s bandwidth at 4 MiB message size and
approximately 1.7 μs small-message latency — solid, representative
evidence the multi-node MPI path performs as designed. The platform is
now in active researcher onboarding.
